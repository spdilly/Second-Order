ILLUSTRATIVE STUDY · PRICING & REVENUE MANAGEMENT · INTERCITY COACH

The study models $41.8M in annual revenue lift on a synthetic $712M coach network.

A SAMPLE STUDY · NATIONAL INTERCITY COACH OPERATOR · SYNTHETIC NETWORK DATA

This illustrative study uses a synthetic network based on the public structure of the US intercity coach industry. All figures are model assumptions or outputs; the revenue lift has not been realized by a client.

The synthetic network

The synthetic network comprises roughly 4,100 origin-destination pairs, with $712M in annual ticket revenue across 16.4M passenger trips and an average fare of $43. Fifty city pairs, the trunk corridors between major metros, account for 54 percent of revenue. The next 350 pairs account for 31 percent. The remaining 3,700 pairs account for 15 percent, and most sell fewer than a dozen seats a day.

The baseline assumes fares set by mileage band and reviewed annually. Trips of a given distance cost roughly the same despite differences in demand by corridor, day, and departure time.

The study considers competition from dynamic pricing on dense corridors. Promotional advance fares can attract price-sensitive buyers, while higher fares apply to the remaining seats near departure.

The pricing constraint

The study tests additional revenue subject to an unchanged day-of station fare. It assumes a quarter of tickets are bought at the station on the day of travel, including riders with limited alternatives.

The model assumes a price elasticity near negative 0.4 for day-of walk-up demand and near negative 1.6 for online advance purchasers comparing coach operators and rail. With the day-of fare unchanged, additional revenue depends on advance pricing, purchase timing, and capacity for demand above the available seats.

The modeled changes

The study assigns pricing methods according to booking volume. Dense corridors provide enough booking history to forecast demand by departure. On a route selling nine seats a day, weekly variation makes that forecast less reliable. The model applies booking-curve pricing to the 50 trunk pairs, seasonal and day-of-week tables to the 350 secondary pairs, and stable published fares to the 3,700 tail pairs.

The model also evaluates the revenue displaced by a short journey on a multi-stop schedule. In the illustrative Washington to Charlotte departure, the last seat out of Washington can sell to Richmond for $25 or to Charlotte for $62. Accepting the short trip prevents the through sale when the shared leg is full, a $37 difference. Availability controls compare these alternatives before accepting a booking.

Added capacity is evaluated alongside peak pricing. The model assumes about $1,100 to add a vehicle and driver to a departure. At 78 forecast riders, 50 seats, and a $45 posted fare, filling one bus at $65 contributes $3,250, while a second section at the posted fare contributes $2,410. Above 97 forecast riders, the modeled contribution from the second section exceeds the pricing alternative. The study uses that crossover as a dispatch threshold for joint pricing and capacity decisions.

The synthetic network assumes average trunk load factors of 84 percent on Fridays and 81 percent on Sundays, against a system average of 61 percent. It models 6.2 percent of trunk departures selling out two or more days early, leaving an estimated 410,000 annual trips unserved, equivalent to about $19M at prevailing fares.

The proposed fare structure increases online advance discounts while retaining the undiscounted day-of base fare. Seat selection and a flexible-fare option are modeled as optional paid additions.

Tail fares remain published and stable between reviews. The study evaluates changes using total network contribution, including revenue from connecting journeys. Approximately 340 tail pairs receive modeled price changes, mostly reductions or the introduction of through-fares.

The modeled result

The five workstreams produce $41.8M of modeled annual revenue lift, 5.9 percent of the base, with separate elasticity assumptions and sensitivities. Booking-curve pricing contributes $10.8M, the largest component. Capturing half of the unserved peak demand through second sections and peak fares contributes $9.6M. The revised fare structure contributes $7.9M, optional additions $8.7M, and tail repricing based on network contribution $4.8M. The day-of walk-up fare remains unchanged.

In this study, booking volume determines the pricing method, while forecast peak demand determines whether an added vehicle produces more contribution than a higher fare.
