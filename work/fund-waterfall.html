Whole-fund distribution timing

Timing determines who receives the profit

Three distribution schedules produce the same 1.5x gross multiple. Their differences reveal how liquidity, preferred return and carried interest divide the value of an investment.

A 1.5x multiple leaves the distribution story unfinished

An investment returns $7.5 million against $5 million contributed. The $2.5 million profit is clear. The investor's economic experience remains unresolved: when the cash arrives affects how long capital is committed, how much profit goes to the limited partner and how much reaches the general partner as carried interest. Timing enters the allocation calculation itself.

This illustrative whole-fund model holds contributions, gross proceeds and distribution terms constant across three schedules. The early schedule returns $5 million in year two and distributes the remaining $2.5 million in year three. The middle schedule pays everything in year four; the late schedule pays everything in year six. These are constructed cash flows, not realized client results.

The schedules separate three questions that a headline multiple compresses: how much the investment generates, how that value is divided and when each party can use its cash. Holding gross proceeds constant makes the effect of timing visible. The same 1.5x at the fund level can lead to different LP multiples and different carry, even before fees and taxes enter the picture.

Returned capital changes the balance that earns preference

The model pays LP capital first, then an 8% effective annual preferred return, compounded quarterly on unreturned capital and unpaid preference. That second balance matters. When the early schedule returns all $5 million of capital in year two, it ends further preference accrual on that capital. It does not erase the $832,000 of preference already accumulated. That unpaid amount continues accruing for another year, reaching $898,560 when the profit distribution arrives.

The middle schedule leaves the full capital contribution outstanding for four years and accumulates $1.80 million of preference. By year six, the late schedule has accumulated approximately $2.93 million, exceeding the available $2.5 million profit. The preference is therefore an economic claim that grows while capital and unpaid preference remain outstanding. A model that stops all accrual when principal is returned misses the surviving preference balance. A model that keeps accruing on the original contribution after repayment overstates it. Neither error is visible in gross MOIC.

The catch-up can preserve carry as preference grows

The early and middle schedules illustrate a less obvious result: a larger preferred-return payment does not necessarily reduce total carry. After satisfying the preference, this waterfall gives the GP 100% of the next proceeds until its share reaches 20% of cumulative distributed profit. Remaining profit then splits 80% to the LP and 20% to the GP. That catch-up can restore the same final profit split despite a different path through the tiers.

In the early schedule, the GP receives $224,640 through catch-up and $275,360 from the residual split: $500,000 in total. In the middle schedule, catch-up rises to $450,611 while the GP's residual falls to $49,389. Total carry remains $500,000. LP profit likewise remains $2 million. More of it is now labeled preferred return and less residual profit; the final allocation has not changed.

The late schedule crosses a different boundary. All $2.5 million of profit goes toward the LP preference, leaving no proceeds for catch-up or residual distributions. Carry falls to zero, and LP profit rises by $500,000. The allocation chart shows both effects: timing first changes the composition of each party's profit, then changes how much profit each receives.

A higher LP multiple can accompany a lower annual return

The early and middle schedules each distribute $7 million to the LP, equivalent to 1.40x contributed capital after carry. The late schedule distributes $7.5 million, or 1.50x, because the GP receives no carry. Yet annualized LP IRR falls from 15.97% in the early schedule to 8.78% in the middle schedule and 6.99% in the late schedule. Receiving more total dollars has not compensated for the additional time in this return measure.

Each figure answers a different question. The distributed multiple records dollars received relative to dollars contributed. IRR reflects their timing. The distribution schedule shows when the LP regains liquidity: the early case returns the entire original contribution four years before the late case. Its higher IRR does not establish what that returned cash subsequently earns. Reinvestment opportunities, cash needs and exposure to the remaining investment would matter to an actual allocation decision; none is estimated here.

The flat part of the curve marks a transfer of profit

A separate sensitivity puts all $7.5 million into a single terminal distribution and moves its date one quarter at a time. This differs from the early schedule, which returns principal before profit. The terminal sensitivity exposes the transition between a fully caught-up GP, a partly caught-up GP and a GP receiving no carry.

At year four, the LP's annualized return is 8.78%. Across the displayed dates from year 4.5 through year 5.25, it holds at 8%. During this interval, the increasing preference directs more of the fixed profit pool to the LP and leaves less for the GP's catch-up. The LP receives enough additional cash at each later date to maintain the modeled preferred rate, while the GP absorbs the reduction. The GP's share shrinks from approximately $430,692 at year 4.5 to $10,640 at year 5.25, while LP IRR is unchanged. A flat LP return curve can therefore conceal a substantial change in the division of profit.

At year 5.5, LP IRR falls to 7.65%; by year six it is 6.99%. Available profit can no longer cover the accrued preference, and carry has already been exhausted. At year six, the approximately $434,372 difference between accrued preference and the $2.5 million paid toward it is an unpaid model balance. It creates neither cash nor a guaranteed recovery.

An exit decision needs a proceeds case and a timing case

Keeping proceeds fixed isolates the economic cost of delay. An actual hold decision would also need to explain the additional proceeds expected from waiting, the capital required to obtain them and the risk of failing to realize them. The fact that time erodes carry is insufficient grounds for an earlier exit. An increase in expected gross proceeds is also incomplete without tracing the resulting cash through the waterfall.

For a decision under these terms, the useful comparison would put an earlier distribution beside a later, explicitly supported proceeds estimate. Each case would show LP dollars, LP timing, annualized return and GP carry. Changes in operating value, financing and distributions could then be distinguished from changes caused solely by the allocation terms. This prevents a discussion of total investment value from obscuring a different question about who receives it.

This simplified example excludes fees, taxes, GP capital, recycling, clawback, equalization and later contributions. Actual governing terms may produce different allocations. Within the stated assumptions, the conclusion is precise: the date of a distribution is part of its economics. A multiple states the amount generated; the cash-flow schedule and waterfall determine how that amount becomes value for each participant.

The assumptions and exclusions below define the worked scenario.

Model notes and assumptions
  • $5 million LP contribution at time zero; no GP capital; $7.5 million gross distributions in every case (1.5x gross MOIC).
  • 8% effective annual preference compounded quarterly on unreturned capital plus unpaid preference; accrual occurs before end-quarter distributions.
  • Tier order: LP capital, accrued LP preference, 100% GP catch-up until GP receives 20% of cumulative distributed profit, then residual 80% LP / 20% GP.
  • Catch-up target = 20% / 80% of cumulative LP profit less GP profit already distributed.
  • LP IRR solves quarterly cash flows and annualizes as (1 + quarterly IRR)^4 - 1.
  • Early: $5m quarter 8 and $2.5m quarter 12; middle: $7.5m quarter 16; late: $7.5m quarter 24.

No fees, taxes, GP commitment, recycling, clawback, equalization or later contributions.

Unpaid preference is a model balance, not a representation of an enforceable guaranteed return.

Unpaid preference continues accruing through the displayed model horizon even after the last distribution; it is not additional available proceeds.

Capital tracked here is unreturned contributed capital, not GAAP or tax-basis capital accounts. Actual governing fund terms may differ.
