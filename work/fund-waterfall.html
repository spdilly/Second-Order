Whole-fund distribution timing

Distribution timing changes the allocation of fund profit

In this illustrative whole-fund waterfall, delayed distributions increase the LP's accrued preference and can eliminate GP carry. The resulting increase in the LP's distributed multiple accompanies a lower annualized return.

Exit timing and investor proceeds

A fund manager's choice between an earlier exit and a longer holding period can change the profit allocated to investors. In the whole-fund waterfall examined here, an unpaid preferred return accrues to the limited partner (LP). Profit is available to the general partner (GP) as carried interest only after the LP receives its capital and accrued preference. Delaying a distribution increases the amount payable to the LP under that priority and can reduce the GP's share.

The analysis assumes a $5 million LP contribution at inception and $7.5 million of gross fund distributions under three schedules. The early schedule returns $5 million in year two and distributes $2.5 million in year three, while the middle schedule pays all proceeds in year four. The late schedule defers the entire distribution until year six. Each produces $2.5 million of fund profit and a 1.5x gross multiple of invested capital (MOIC).

Gross proceeds remain fixed at $7.5 million throughout the comparison, isolating the effect of distribution timing on the waterfall. The analysis assumes no increase in asset value during the additional holding period.

Preference accrual after capital repayment

The modeled preferred return accrues at an 8% effective annual rate, compounded quarterly on unreturned capital and unpaid preference. Accrual precedes each end-quarter distribution, and available cash first repays LP capital. In the early schedule, the $5 million payment at the end of year two repays the full contribution and leaves $832,000 of accrued preference unpaid.

Over the following year, preference accrues on that $832,000 balance, increasing it to $898,560 by the profit distribution in year three. The repaid capital no longer earns preference. Calculating the payment therefore requires separate balances for unreturned contributions and unpaid preference. Ending all accrual at capital repayment would understate the LP's preference, while continuing to accrue on the original $5 million would overstate it.

In the middle schedule, the contribution remains outstanding for four years and accumulates $1.80 million of preference. Six years without a distribution increases the late schedule's accrued balance to approximately $2.93 million, exceeding the $2.5 million of available profit.

Catch-up payments and carried interest

After the LP receives its preference, the waterfall allocates all further proceeds to the GP until it has received 20% of cumulative distributed profit. This GP catch-up is followed by an 80% LP and 20% GP split of any residual profit. When proceeds are sufficient to complete the catch-up, a larger preference changes the payments through the individual tiers while preserving the final profit allocation.

The early schedule pays the GP $224,640 through catch-up and $275,360 from the residual split. In the middle schedule, those payments change to $450,611 and $49,389, respectively. Both schedules allocate $500,000 of total carry to the GP and $2 million of profit to the LP. The additional preference in the middle schedule reduces the LP's residual distribution by an equal amount.

At year six, all $2.5 million of profit is applied to the LP preference. The remaining accrued preference cannot be paid, so the waterfall allocates nothing to GP catch-up or residual distributions. Relative to the early and middle schedules, the LP receives $500,000 more profit and the GP receives $500,000 less.

LP distributions and annualized returns

Including capital repayment, the early and middle schedules each distribute $7 million to the LP, equivalent to 1.40x contributed capital after carry. The late schedule distributes $7.5 million, or 1.50x. Annualized LP internal rate of return (IRR), which accounts for the dates of the cash flows, is 15.97% in the early schedule, 8.78% in the middle schedule and 6.99% in the late schedule. These returns exclude fees and taxes.

The late schedule's additional $500,000 raises the LP's distributed multiple, but its six-year holding period produces the lowest annualized return. The early schedule returns the entire original contribution four years sooner, making that capital available for other uses while the final profit distribution remains outstanding. Its higher IRR measures the modeled fund cash flows and excludes subsequent returns on the cash received. An investor evaluating the schedules would also have to assess reinvestment opportunities and its own cash requirements, together with the risk attached to proceeds still expected from the fund.

Partial catch-up and the preferred return

A separate sensitivity assumes a single $7.5 million distribution and changes its date in quarterly increments. Capital is repaid with profit at the terminal date in every case. This differs from the early schedule above, which repays the contribution a year before distributing profit.

At year four, the LP's annualized return is 8.78%. At the displayed dates from year 4.5 through year 5.25, it remains at 8% because the LP receives its accrued preference in full, while proceeds cover only part of the GP catch-up. With each additional quarter, more profit is allocated to the growing preference balance. The GP's payment declines from approximately $430,692 at year 4.5 to $10,640 at year 5.25. During this interval, the additional dollars received by the LP offset the delay in payment at an annualized rate of 8%, funded by the reduction in GP carry.

By year 5.5, the preference exceeds the $2.5 million profit available, leaving no carry and reducing LP IRR to 7.65%. At year six, LP IRR is 6.99%, with approximately $434,372 of accrued preference unpaid after the distribution. That balance records the shortfall against the modeled preference. Its continued accrual does not increase available proceeds or establish a guaranteed recovery.

Proceeds assumptions in the exit decision

The fixed-proceeds analysis shows the allocation consequences of delay under the stated terms. It supplies no estimate of the asset appreciation that might justify a longer hold. Evaluating a later exit would require a proceeds estimate supported by the operating plan, together with the additional capital needed during the holding period and the risk of achieving the projected sale price. Any change to those assumptions would require new cash flows and a recalculation of the waterfall.

For an exit comparison under these terms, each proposed sale date would have an associated LP distribution schedule and carried-interest calculation. The comparison would distinguish changes in gross fund profit from changes in the portion allocated to the GP, with LP repayment dates and annualized returns reported for each case. Selecting an exit date requires comparing expected investment proceeds and their risk alongside the allocation of profit to the GP.

The example excludes fees, taxes, GP capital, recycling, clawback, equalization and later contributions. Applying it to a fund would require replacing these simplifications with the governing terms and the fund's contribution and distribution history.

The assumptions and exclusions below define the worked scenario.

Model notes and assumptions
  • $5 million LP contribution at time zero; no GP capital; $7.5 million gross distributions in every case (1.5x gross MOIC).
  • 8% effective annual preference compounded quarterly on unreturned capital plus unpaid preference; accrual occurs before end-quarter distributions.
  • Tier order: LP capital, accrued LP preference, 100% GP catch-up until GP receives 20% of cumulative distributed profit, then residual 80% LP / 20% GP.
  • Catch-up target = 20% / 80% of cumulative LP profit less GP profit already distributed.
  • LP IRR solves quarterly cash flows and annualizes as (1 + quarterly IRR)^4 - 1.
  • Early: $5m quarter 8 and $2.5m quarter 12; middle: $7.5m quarter 16; late: $7.5m quarter 24.

No fees, taxes, GP commitment, recycling, clawback, equalization or later contributions.

Unpaid preference is a model balance, not a representation of an enforceable guaranteed return.

Unpaid preference continues accruing through the displayed model horizon even after the last distribution; it is not additional available proceeds.

Capital tracked here is unreturned contributed capital, not GAAP or tax-basis capital accounts. Actual governing fund terms may differ.
