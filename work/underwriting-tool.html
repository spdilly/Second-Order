Single-family rental underwriting

The down payment is only the first cash decision

A rental's steady-state yield says little about the cash its owner may need between tenants. The financing decision has to survive that interval.

The investment committee should follow the cash past closing

Consider an illustrative $180,000 rental with $1,900 monthly rent. The investment requires more than the down payment: closing costs, initial rehabilitation and a funded reserve add $21,400. Changing the loan changes the equity check and debt service, but it does not improve the property, its rent or its operating costs. That distinction matters when a financing proposal appears to make an otherwise ordinary asset more attractive.

The useful question is how much capital the owner must commit to carry the property through an operating interruption. Some is spent at closing. Some sits in reserve. Some may have to arrive later, when rent has stopped and a repair invoice is due. A decision based only on the first check can understate the commitment even when the annual forecast is internally consistent.

This worked scenario examines that commitment in two ways: a steady-state rent sensitivity and a separate monthly vacancy-and-repair event. Neither represents a realized client result or a prediction of how often the event will occur. Together, they show why acquisition equity, earning power and access to cash deserve separate decisions.

A smaller equity check can leave a much smaller income stream

At the base $1,900 monthly rent, 75% loan-to-value produces a 2.65% annual cash-on-cash yield. Moving to 85% reduces that yield to 0.52%. The denominator falls, but the remaining cash falls faster: annual cash after debt declines from about $1,760 to $250. These yields include the funded reserve in invested cash and exclude appreciation and principal amortization from the return numerator.

The higher loan saves $18,000 of acquisition equity while absorbing about $1,510 more cash each year through debt service. That is the immediate financing tradeoff. An owner choosing the larger loan is preserving cash outside the property in exchange for a thinner stream inside it. Whether that is sensible depends partly on whether the preserved cash will remain available when the property needs it.

The sensitivity also shows where the attraction of leverage emerges. At $2,300 rent, the modeled cash yields converge: 8.46% without debt and 8.69% at 85% leverage. At $1,900, they are 6.50% and 0.52%. The financing choice therefore depends heavily on the rent the property can actually sustain. A higher assumed rent can make leverage look productive without changing the contractual payment that remains due if the assumption disappoints.

A tenant departure can hit both sides of the account

Vacancy and repairs are often separate lines in an underwriting model. They can still be consequences of the same event. A departure may interrupt collections while creating work that must be completed before the next tenant moves in. Testing each line in isolation can miss the cash requirement of that sequence; adding them together does not require assuming an extreme outcome for either one.

At a monthly rent of $1,805, 5% below base, the 75% financing case retains about $1,020 of annual cash after debt with 4% vacancy and $1,500 of repairs. Raising vacancy to 10% makes that balance slightly negative, at approximately $176 below zero. Keeping vacancy at 4% but increasing repairs to $4,500 produces a roughly $1,980 deficit. Combining 10% vacancy with $4,500 repairs takes the deficit to about $3,176.

The repair increase alone consumes $3,000, nearly three times the first scenario's remaining cash. This is the practical limit hidden behind a modestly positive forecast: the margin has already been allocated to several potential claims. That exposure belongs in the initial funding decision. The scenarios establish the cash required under each combination; they do not estimate the probability of an owner having to supply it.

The reserve reaches its limit in month five

The monthly test starts each financing structure with the same $6,000 reserve. Rent is fully collected in ordinary months, absent in months four and five, and accompanied by an additional $4,500 repair invoice in month four. Routine repairs and capital reserves continue to be funded. This is a separate cash-path test, not the annual vacancy sensitivity spread across twelve months.

By the end of month five, the 60% loan-to-value case has $118 left. The 75% case has a funding gap of $826; the 85% case needs $1,455. Those negative balances describe additional owner funding required to meet the modeled cash outflows. The chart deliberately inserts no owner top-up, so the underlying shortfall remains visible.

The 60% structure clears zero, but that is a narrow technical distinction. Its $118 balance provides almost no room for a further expense before the next collection. An approval standard that asks only whether the reserve becomes negative would call this case sufficient. An investment committee should also specify the cash buffer it wants to retain at the low point, then fund the difference. The minimum required to avoid a shortfall and the reserve an owner would feel comfortable relying on are different amounts.

Returning above zero does not restore the ability to absorb another shock

Recovery speed changes the meaning of the initial deficit. After the month-five trough, the modeled 75% case returns above zero in month nine. The 85% case does not do so until month nineteen. The first property has four months of negative month-end balances; the second has fourteen. A relatively small difference in the initial funding gap creates a much longer period of dependence on outside cash.

Month nineteen reveals the wider separation. The 60% structure has rebuilt its original $6,000 reserve. The 85% structure has only just returned above zero, with about $60. Its ordinary monthly surplus is approximately $108, compared with $423 at 60% leverage. Both resume collecting the same rent, but very different amounts remain to repair the balance sheet.

Even at month twenty-four, the 75% reserve is only about $3,621 and the 85% reserve about $601. Neither has returned to its starting level. The investment remains exposed after the visible operating problem has ended. Underwriting should therefore follow the rebuilding period as carefully as the interruption itself, particularly if the owner plans to distribute cash soon after collections resume.

Financeable price follows the owner's capacity to carry the property

The decision is a combination of purchase price, debt and committed liquidity. A lower price can reduce the loan balance required at a given loan-to-value. A smaller loan can improve the monthly cash available to rebuild reserves. A larger opening reserve can cover the modeled trough, but it does not improve the property's underlying earning power. Each response solves a different part of the problem.

For this scenario, simply adding $826 to the 75% opening reserve would cover the observed shortfall to rounding. It would not establish an adequate buffer for a second event. That is why reserve sizing should start with an explicit minimum balance and an acceptable rebuilding period. The offer price and financing can then be tested against those requirements, rather than selected first and justified with an optimistic rent assumption.

A financeable purchase is one the owner can fund through the proposed operating path. The closing check, the lowest cash balance and the time needed to rebuild reserves belong in the same approval discussion. A return forecast becomes more useful when it shows both what the asset may earn and what the owner must remain prepared to supply.

The assumptions and exclusions below define the worked scenario.

Model notes and assumptions
  • $180,000 purchase price, $5,400 closing costs, $10,000 initial rehabilitation and $6,000 funded reserve.
  • Illustrative 7.5% fixed note, 30-year monthly amortization, loan amount based only on purchase price.
  • $1,900 base monthly rent; 5% vacancy; management 8% of collected rent; $4,200 annual taxes and insurance; $1,500 routine repairs; capital reserve 5% of scheduled rent.
  • Cash yield = annual cash after debt / down payment plus closing costs, rehabilitation and initial reserve; principal amortization and appreciation excluded from the numerator.
  • Monthly liquidity case: no rent in months 4 and 5, extra $4,500 repair in month 4; all other months fully collected; no rent growth.

Rates, rents, costs and leverage are scenario inputs, not current lender quotes or HUD payment standards.

No taxes on investor income, sale proceeds, refinancing, utilities or security deposits modeled.

Monthly shock case is separate from steady-state vacancy sensitivity and does not imply a probability.
