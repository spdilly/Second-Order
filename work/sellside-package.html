Sale preparation · Business services

Earnings at sale. Cash after closing.

A defensible earnings number helps establish the sale price. The growth plan and the working-capital agreement determine how much cash changes hands, and how much the buyer still needs.

The seller's earnings bridge becomes the buyer's operating budget.

In this illustrative business-services sale, the owner proposes $4.38m of adjusted EBITDA against $3.20m reported. The adjustments describe costs the seller believes a new owner can avoid. Accepting them therefore requires a view of the business after the transaction: which costs disappear, which return under another name, and which are necessary to deliver the revenue being valued?

The model accepts a $0.40m legal settlement, $0.35m of excess owner compensation and a $0.18m ERP implementation expense. Each acceptance is conditional on evidence. The settlement needs to be nonrecurring; the compensation adjustment must leave enough to replace the owner's responsibilities; and ongoing system costs must remain in the operating budget. An invoice labelled 'project' cannot establish that distinction by itself.

Recurring temporary labor fails this test. The buyer would continue spending the proposed $0.25m adjustment to operate the business, leaving $4.13m of maintainable EBITDA. At the example's fixed 6.0× multiple, accepting or rejecting that single item changes enterprise value by $1.50m. Establishing a credible earnings base is already a price negotiation, before anyone argues about the multiple.

The growth plan introduces a second claim on cash.

Next, the buyer considers 25% revenue growth, from $28m to $35m, at the same 14.75% EBITDA margin. Earnings rise by $1.03m to $5.16m. Even with collections unchanged at 55 days, the larger business produces less annual cash after tax, maintenance spending and working-capital investment: $2.11m against approximately $2.43m with flat sales.

The explanation sits in the balance sheet. Growth alone requires $1.05m of additional receivables. The modeled inventory and payable movements offset approximately $0.12m, leaving $0.93m of additional net working capital. Meanwhile, the earnings increase contributes only $0.61m after incremental cash tax and maintenance capex. The difference reduces cash available by approximately $323,000.

That funding requirement belongs in the growth decision. A buyer planning debt service or distributions from EBITDA would miss it. It also needs a time boundary: the $0.93m is the investment required to move to the larger revenue base in this forward year. If sales and collection terms then hold steady, the model would not require the same incremental investment again. Continued growth, however, would require another calculation.

One collection day is worth approximately $96,000.

At $35m of annual sales, each extra collection day holds approximately $95,890 in receivables. Moving from 55 to 72 days absorbs a further $1.63m, on top of the $0.93m required by growth at unchanged collection terms. Total incremental working capital reaches $2.56m. The same $5.16m of EBITDA now leaves just $0.48m of annual cash before acquisition financing and distributions.

The collection curves show how quickly the room for error narrows. Annual cash reaches zero at approximately 86.7 collection days with flat sales, 77.0 days with 25% growth and 72.9 days with 40% growth. These are thresholds within the stated assumptions, before interest, debt amortization or additional growth capex. A financed acquisition could need considerably more headroom.

There is also a useful operating target inside the calculation. At 25% growth, improving collections from 55 days to approximately 51.6 days would preserve the annual cash generated by the flat-sales case. That translates the growth ambition into a specific cash requirement. Whether those days can be recovered depends on billing and customer behavior; the arithmetic establishes their value, not their availability.

An aging schedule has to explain what the customer owes.

A 72-day collection cycle identifies a funding problem without explaining its cause. An agreed extended payment term, an invoice awaiting customer acceptance and a disputed charge can occupy the same aging bucket while carrying very different collection prospects. The review should follow material balances through invoice dates, contractual terms, disputes, credits and subsequent receipts.

This changes the negotiation. A collectible invoice that takes longer to settle can remain an asset while increasing the buyer's liquidity requirement. A balance that requires a credit or cannot be collected raises a separate question about the amount recognized. Applying a blanket deduction for every overdue invoice would conflate those questions; accepting every balance at face value would leave the evidence untested.

Concentration matters as well. An average improvement in collection days can conceal one large unresolved balance. The sale preparation should identify which customers account for the additional receivables and whether the growth forecast relies on extending their terms further. That connects the commercial plan to the assets the buyer will actually receive.

The same receivables can increase the seller's closing payment.

The closing illustration values the business at $24.78m, using $4.13m of maintainable EBITDA and the fixed 6.0× multiple. It deliberately keeps the projected $5.16m of EBITDA out of this price calculation. The growth scenario tests funding; it does not silently reprice the transaction.

The agreed working-capital target is modeled at 55 collection days on $35m of sales. Actual working capital reflects 72 days. If the additional receivables are collectible and qualify under the agreed definitions, the $1.63m excess above target increases the seller's payment. After $8.40m of net debt and $0.65m of fees, the reconciliation produces $17.36m for the seller before owner-level taxes and any unmodeled escrow or holdback.

Slower collection therefore does not automatically produce a seller debit. Here the buyer pays for more receivables at closing and waits longer to collect them. The result depends on both the target and the eligible balances. The $1.63m adjustment measures the 17-day difference at the same sales level; it excludes the $0.93m growth investment already embedded in the target. Changing that target would change the reconciliation.

A buyer must place the funding requirement on the right date.

The forward-year cash calculation and the closing reconciliation are two views of the example, not amounts to add together mechanically. If the slowdown happens after acquisition, the buyer funds the receivables as they build. If those same receivables already exist at closing and are purchased through the working-capital adjustment, they form part of the opening balance sheet.

In the latter case, the buyer's post-closing forecast must start from the acquired balances. Charging the $1.63m closing surplus and then treating that same buildup as a new post-closing cash outflow would count it twice. A dated sources-and-uses schedule and a monthly opening balance sheet resolve the boundary more reliably than a single annual cash figure.

The annual model also cannot establish the peak funding need. Payroll dates, customer receipts and transaction payments may create a tighter month than the year-end result suggests. Financing capacity should be tested against that timing before the buyer treats the residual cash as available for distributions.

Preparation should settle the evidence before it reaches the price.

The seller's package should allow a buyer to trace three decisions: the cost of running the business under new ownership, the investment needed to deliver the growth plan, and the working capital included in the closing payment. Each needs its own supporting schedule, with consistent revenue assumptions and dates.

For this example, the priority evidence is replacement compensation, the ongoing labor requirement, invoice-level collection support and the bridge from target to actual working capital. Those items determine whether the proposed earnings base and cash reconciliation can withstand review. A clean presentation cannot resolve an unsupported adjustment; a well-supported receivable need not become a price concession simply because collection is slow.

The assumptions and exclusions below define the worked scenario.

Model notes and assumptions
  • Opening revenue $28m; reported EBITDA $3.20m. Accepted adjustments are $0.40m legal settlement, $0.35m owner compensation, and $0.18m ERP implementation. A $0.25m recurring labor adjustment is rejected.
  • Normalized EBITDA margin is held at 14.75%. Cost of goods sold is 64% of sales; inventory and payable days are 20 and 30. Opening receivable days are 55.
  • Maintenance capex is $0.80m and depreciation $0.55m at opening revenue, each growing with sales. Cash tax is 25% of positive EBIT; no interest, growth capex, or tax loss carryforward is included.
  • Cash available = EBITDA − cash tax − maintenance capex − increase in net working capital. All cases represent a single forward year.
  • Sale value uses a fixed 6.0× multiple, $8.40m net debt, $0.65m fees, and a working-capital target at 55 collection days on $35m sales. The seller-cash bridge adds actual working capital above the target; all receivables are assumed collectible and included at face value.

No client outcome is represented. Add-back acceptance, financing, and closing adjustments depend on the actual evidence and agreement.
