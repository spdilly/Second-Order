Power markets · Valuation

The obligation behind the power price.

A long-term power contract can make revenue more predictable while making an outage more expensive. Value depends on which risks the seller still owns.

Constellation’s September 2024 announcement of a 20-year agreement with Microsoft illustrates the scale of long-term nuclear power contracting. The company announcement described an approximately 835 MW restart. The worked analysis below uses a separate 1,000 MW plant to examine contract structure.

A fixed price is only part of the bargain.

A twenty-year power agreement invites a simple valuation story: secure a large customer, stabilize revenue, and finance the asset more cheaply. But its value depends on the promised product. Buying electricity when a plant produces it differs substantially from receiving electricity whether that plant operates or not. The analysis below uses an illustrative 1,000 MW plant and assumed contract terms to examine that distinction. It does not estimate the economics of Constellation's Microsoft agreement.

The assumed plant produces 7.884 million MWh a year at 90% availability. An 80% contract at $65/MWh can describe either a share of actual generation or a fixed annual obligation of 6.3072 million MWh. Those alternatives produce identical cash in the expected operating year. An ordinary base-case valuation therefore cannot tell them apart. Their difference emerges when the plant misses its production plan.

Price protection has an observable cost.

Start with the as-generated agreement, which pays $65/MWh on 80% of actual output and leaves 20% exposed to the wholesale market. At the assumed 90% availability, every $1/MWh change in the market price changes annual revenue by $1.58 million, compared with $7.88 million for a fully merchant plant. The contract removes four-fifths of this price sensitivity; it does not remove the plant's exposure to output, operating costs, or customer payment.

At a $30/MWh market price, annual cash after operating costs, maintenance capital expenditure, and modeled tax improves from a $90.6 million outflow to a $97.6 million inflow. That is economically consequential protection. It could preserve the ability to fund maintenance through a weak market. At $100/MWh, however, cash falls from $346.0 million in the merchant case to $180.4 million under the contract. The same agreement that supports the downside transfers much of the upside to the buyer.

The lines cross at $65/MWh because that is the agreed price. Calling the agreement attractive requires a judgment about the price distribution, the owner's ability to withstand low-price periods, and the value it places on foregone upside. A smaller range of outcomes is useful, but it is not itself proof of a higher asset value.

Below 72% availability, the hedge changes direction.

Now fix the delivery obligation at 6.3072 million MWh, regardless of actual generation. Production above that quantity sells at the market price; a shortfall must be purchased at that price. The critical boundary is 72% availability, equal to 80% of the 90% production assumption. Above it, the plant still benefits from higher wholesale prices. Below it, higher prices make the delivery promise more expensive to fulfill.

At 55% availability, production is only 4.818 million MWh. The seller must buy 1.4892 million MWh to meet its obligation. At a $75/MWh market price, modeled annual cash is positive $35.9 million. At $150/MWh, it becomes negative $63.9 million. The market has strengthened while the contracted plant's cash position has deteriorated by almost $100 million. At 90% availability, the same price increase improves cash because the plant has surplus power to sell.

At 70% availability, the shortfall is much smaller: 175,200 MWh. Price exposure has already reversed, even though modeled cash stays positive throughout the plotted range. The threshold marks a change in the direction of risk; it is not the point at which the plant becomes unprofitable. That distinction matters when designing operating triggers and contract limits.

The downward-sloping lines are the relevant warning. A contract can protect against a weak power market and still expose its owner to an expensive outage. There is no single contracted percentage that communicates both effects. The quantity obligation and the operating condition have to appear alongside it.

The expensive hours matter more than the average year.

This annual model deliberately settles every surplus or shortfall at one price. Real delivery analysis needs the settlement periods specified in the agreement. Two operating years with the same total generation can have different economics if one loses output during cheap hours and the other loses it during scarcity. An annual production surplus does not necessarily cover a contractual shortfall in a particular hour. Buying power in expensive hours and selling surplus in cheaper hours creates a loss that an annual average can conceal.

A useful stress case therefore combines plant unavailability with high replacement prices. That is a scenario to test, not a forecast that the two will always coincide. The danger is greater where the event interrupting production also strains the wider grid: treating outage probability and replacement cost as independent can understate the exposure.

This changes the commercial discussion. Outage allowances, delivery schedules, settlement location, and limits on replacement obligations affect the price a seller should accept. A higher headline price may simply compensate the generator for taking on a harder delivery promise. Comparing prices without those terms risks comparing different products.

The discount-rate benefit must earn its place.

At the model's $70/MWh wholesale forecast, the as-generated contract reduces annual cash from $168.6 million to $144.9 million. The $23.7 million reduction is the after-tax cost of selling 80% of generation at a $5/MWh discount. Over twenty years, with level annual cash and no terminal value, it reduces present value by $208.4 million at an unchanged 9.5% discount rate.

Reducing the contracted-case rate to 7.5% adds back $200.3 million. Contracted present value reaches $1,477.5 million, compared with $1,485.6 million for the merchant case. The assumed rate reduction almost pays for the price concession, but does not quite do so. That near-offset is a result of these inputs, not evidence that two percentage points is the correct adjustment for a power contract.

The rate has to follow the remaining exposure. Customer credit, payment security, delivery obligations, and operating risk all affect how dependable the cash actually is. Contract-specific expected losses belong in the cash forecast before deciding how to discount it. A favorable rate cannot be selected merely because it restores the value surrendered through price. Nor should an analyst count the same reduction in risk both as a higher cash forecast and an unexplained valuation premium.

A financing case needs one further distinction. More predictable receipts may support cheaper borrowing or additional debt capacity, but neither establishes an equal reduction in the discount rate for all asset cash flows. This example contains no debt. Financing benefits would require a separate analysis of borrowing terms, coverage, and the risk retained by equity.

Energy revenue and capacity value need separate treatment.

The charts value energy cash flows only. Electricity sold in MWh is distinct from capacity: the commitment of resources to be available when the system needs them. PJM's explanation of its capacity market also makes clear that performance obligations accompany capacity payments. A long-term energy price does not, by itself, establish who retains that separate revenue or its associated obligations.

Before adding capacity value, establish the rights retained, the capacity that can qualify, and the performance exposure. Before approving investment, bring the initial capital requirement into the analysis; these operating present values cannot establish a sufficient return for a restart or new plant. The contract negotiation then has a concrete economic basis: the price upside being exchanged, the delivery obligations being retained, and the compensation that makes those choices worthwhile.

The assumptions and exclusions below define the worked scenario.

Model notes and assumptions
  • Plant capacity 1,000 MW; expected availability 90%, producing 7.884m MWh annually. This is an assumed operating case.
  • Variable cost $25/MWh; fixed operating costs plus maintenance capex $130m annually. Cash tax is 25% of positive pre-tax operating cash; negative periods receive no immediate tax benefit.
  • An 80% as-generated PPA pays $65/MWh on 80% of actual generation. The firm-volume comparison instead commits 6.3072m MWh a year; excess production or shortfall settles at the plotted wholesale price.
  • No transmission basis, hourly shape, escalation, capacity payments, production credits, new-build capex, or debt financing is modeled. The example separates price and delivery exposure.
  • The valuation uses 20 end-year cash flows, expected wholesale price $70/MWh, and no terminal value. The discount-rate comparison is 9.5% versus 7.5%; it is an assumption, not a measured market risk premium.

The charts are not a price target or valuation of a named security.
