Power markets · Valuation

The price of contracted power.

A long-term agreement can support investment and reduce price exposure. Delivery obligations determine how much risk remains.

A price commitment reduces both downside and upside.A fixed delivery volume can turn high prices into a liability.The discount-rate benefit has to pay for the price concession.

Constellation’s September 2024 announcement of a 20-year agreement with Microsoft illustrates the scale of long-term nuclear power contracting. The company announcement described an approximately 835 MW restart. The worked analysis below uses a separate 1,000 MW plant to examine contract structure.

A price commitment reduces both downside and upside.

Selling 80% of generation at $65/MWh dampens the effect of wholesale prices. The contract protects operating cash at low prices and gives up revenue when the market is stronger. That exchange needs to be valued before assigning a lower discount rate.

A fixed delivery volume can turn high prices into a liability.

An as-generated agreement sells a share of actual output. A firm-volume agreement commits to a quantity whether the plant produces it or not. Below 72% availability, this plant produces less than its contracted volume and must purchase the difference.

Higher market prices then increase replacement-power costs. A contract that reduces price risk during normal operation can amplify the cost of an outage. Reliability assumptions and delivery terms belong in the same valuation model.

The discount-rate benefit has to pay for the price concession.

With a $70/MWh market forecast, the $65 contract reduces the present value of cash flows by $208m when the discount rate stays at 9.5%. Reducing the contracted-case rate to 7.5% adds $200m. Under those assumptions, the two effects almost offset.

The rate reduction is a sensitivity, not an automatic reward for signing a contract. Counterparty credit, delivery obligations, outages, and the timing of generation determine whether the lower rate is justified.

The public announcement supplies commercial context. The plant economics and contract terms in the charts are a separate worked scenario, not Constellation’s disclosed pricing, cash flow, or valuation.

Model notes and assumptions
  • Plant capacity 1,000 MW; expected availability 90%, producing 7.884m MWh annually. This is an assumed operating case.
  • Variable cost $25/MWh; fixed operating costs plus maintenance capex $130m annually. Cash tax is 25% of positive pre-tax operating cash; negative periods receive no immediate tax benefit.
  • An 80% as-generated PPA pays $65/MWh on 80% of actual generation. The firm-volume comparison instead commits 6.3072m MWh a year; excess production or shortfall settles at the plotted wholesale price.
  • No transmission basis, hourly shape, escalation, capacity payments, production credits, new-build capex, or debt financing is modeled. The example separates price and delivery exposure.
  • The valuation uses 20 end-year cash flows, expected wholesale price $70/MWh, and no terminal value. The discount-rate comparison is 9.5% versus 7.5%; it is an assumption, not a measured market risk premium.

The charts are not a price target or valuation of a named security.
