Growth capital · boutique fitness

Growing a studio without exhausting capacity

A cohort and cash model connects retention, peak-time availability, instructor costs, and the timing of a second location.

Peak availability affects retentionHiring too early and hiring too late carry different costsThe second opening competes with the first studio for cash

Peak availability affects retention

Membership cohorts lose customers each month and receive new joins. Members request eight visits per month, with 60% concentrated in peak periods. Once peak requests exceed scheduled places, the model increases churn in the following month.

With the initial class schedule unchanged, month-36 membership reaches 379. Adding instructor capacity in month 13 supports 534 members by the same date. The extra classes cost more immediately; their retention benefit accumulates over subsequent cohorts.

Hiring too early and hiring too late carry different costs

Earlier staffing adds payroll before the membership base needs all available places. Later staffing leaves more peak requests unserved, which reduces subsequent membership and recurring revenue. The sensitivity recalculates the full cohort schedule for each staffing date and baseline churn assumption.

The measure is cumulative operating cash before opening expenditure, financing, and terminal value. Every staffing date falls within the 36-month horizon; the model charges the expanded instructor schedule from that date onward.

The second opening competes with the first studio for cash

The expansion cases start with $300,000, pay the first studio fit-out in month 1, and fund a $180,000 second fit-out at the selected opening date. The second location has its own acquisition, price, and fixed-cost assumptions while using the same operating calculations.

The lowest month-end balance measures funding pressure during the overlap of two ramps. Opening later allows the first studio more time to generate cash, but delays the second location’s operating contribution. Negative balances show an unfunded requirement.

Worked scenario using the assumptions below. Figures demonstrate the analysis and do not represent a realized client result.

Model notes and assumptions
  • First studio joins 30 members per month for 18 months, then 24; monthly membership price $185.
  • Baseline monthly churn is 3.5%; next-month churn adds 20 percentage points multiplied by the current fraction of unmet peak visits, capped at 20% total churn.
  • Each member requests 8 monthly visits; 60% are peak. A class accommodates 12 members and a month has 4.33 weeks.
  • Initial schedule has 30 peak and 65 total classes per week. Expanded staffing has 48 peak and 90 total classes. Instructor cost is $48 per class.
  • First studio fixed cash cost is $17,000 per month; acquisition cost is $125 per join; payment and variable servicing costs are 3.5% of revenue; fit-out is $140,000.
  • Second studio joins 26 members monthly for 18 months, then 20.8; price is $200; fixed costs $19,000 monthly; fit-out $180,000. Other calculations and instructor costs are unchanged.
  • Combined starting funding is $300,000 and reserve target is $60,000. Each studio expands staffing in operating month 13 for the opening-date comparison.

Scenario assumptions are invented for transparent public analysis; they are not client actuals, management forecasts, or US/UK market benchmarks.

Churn response to unmet peak demand is assumed rather than empirically estimated. Members continue paying the full membership fee despite some unavailable visits; the response operates through subsequent churn.

No tax, VAT, exchange-rate changes, debt service, depreciation, deposits, maintenance capex, refunds, cannibalization between locations, or separate working-capital lag.

Demand and class schedules are aggregate monthly averages. Instructor availability and physical timetable feasibility require operating validation.

Different opening dates yield different second-studio operating ages at month 36; the final cash balance is not a like-for-like lifetime return comparison.

Intramonth payment timing is not modeled; maintaining the reserve throughout a month may require more funding than the month-end shortfall.
