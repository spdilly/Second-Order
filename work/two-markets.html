When a busy studio is ready for a second location

A second lease draws on the same cash that funds the first studio’s timetable. Connecting capacity, member cohorts and opening dates changes the expansion decision.

The next lease starts with a cash question

A studio can have growing membership, a promising second site and too little cash to connect the two. In this worked example, built from illustrative assumptions rather than a realized client engagement, the business starts with $300,000. The first fit-out costs $140,000 and the second $180,000. Trading must therefore fund part of the premises investment as well as the losses incurred while each location builds its membership.

The earliest expansion looks attractive at the end of the forecast. Opening the second studio in month 12 produces $1.213 million of combined cash by month 36, compared with $1.006 million for a month-18 opening. But the earlier case reaches a negative $102,526 in month 14. Its apparent destination depends on financing an interruption along the way.

Avoiding a negative month-end balance would require at least $102,526 beyond the starting funding. Preserving the assumed $60,000 reserve raises that requirement to $162,526, before any financing costs. The first expansion question is whether the business can fund this passage. Understanding the answer requires looking inside the first studio’s operating model.

The binding capacity sits inside the week

The initial timetable offers 65 classes a week, each with 12 places. Across an average month, that creates 3,377 places. Yet only 30 classes fall in peak periods, while members request 60% of their visits then. At eight requested visits per member each month, peak capacity supports about 325 members. Total monthly places could accommodate about 422 members if demand fitted the timetable perfectly.

This distinction makes average utilization a poor expansion trigger. When the peak timetable fills, aggregate requested visits represent only about 77% of all scheduled places. A report showing considerable spare capacity could coexist with members struggling to book the sessions they want. Empty midday places do not automatically substitute for an unavailable evening class.

The model links unmet peak demand to higher churn in the following month. Keeping the initial schedule produces 379 members at month 36. Increasing peak classes from 30 to 48 in month 13 supports 534. The expanded timetable also encounters its peak constraint later in the forecast; adding capacity postpones the problem rather than abolishing it. These are modeled responses to an assumed relationship between availability and retention.

New joins can conceal a replacement treadmill

Membership is a stock built from successive cohorts. Joins and departures are the flows that change it. This matters because a healthy sales report can conceal a business spending increasingly to replace members it has lost. After month 18, the first studio continues acquiring 24 members a month. With the original timetable, membership nevertheless settles near 379: new customers almost exactly replace departures.

At the assumed acquisition cost of $125 per join, that means $3,000 of monthly acquisition spending with almost no net membership growth late in the forecast. The spend still replaces lost revenue, but it does not expand the recurring base. Looking only at acquisition volume would miss the capacity assumption driving this result.

The expanded schedule ends month 36 with roughly 156 additional members at the same acquisition rate. At $185 per membership, that represents approximately $28,800 of additional monthly revenue at that point. It is an ending run rate, not a benefit earned throughout all 36 months. Instructor costs begin immediately; the additional retained membership accumulates gradually. That difference in timing is the investment case for funding retention capacity.

Instructor commitments buy time for cohorts to mature

Expanding the full schedule from 65 to 90 weekly classes adds $5,196 of instructor cost each month. Committing six months earlier therefore spends another $31,176. At the 3.5% baseline monthly churn assumption, moving the staffing increase from month 13 to month 7 reduces cumulative operating cash by exactly that amount: the early classes add cost before relieving a binding constraint.

Waiting longer carries a different cost. Moving the increase from month 13 to month 16 reduces 36-month operating cash from $962,655 to $957,340 despite saving three months of expanded instructor expense. The smaller retained cohorts outweigh the deferred payroll within this forecast. A calendar-based hiring policy cannot resolve both sides of that trade-off.

The staffing-date curves show how retention changes the decision. Among the dates tested, month 13 produces the most operating cash at 2% and 3.5% baseline churn; month 16 leads at 5%; month 31 leads at 6%. A business losing members more quickly builds demand for additional capacity more slowly. The sensitivity does not establish an ideal hiring date: it uses a finite set of dates and a 36-month measure before fit-out, financing and terminal value. Its practical value is showing when the staffing decision depends heavily on evidence about retention.

A later opening buys headroom, with a limit

Once the timetable decision is reflected in cash, the opening alternatives become easier to interpret. Both studios expand staffing in their thirteenth operating month for this comparison. A month-18 second opening reaches a minimum combined month-end balance of $58,567. It avoids a negative balance but misses the $60,000 planning reserve by $1,433. Calling it fully funded against that reserve would overlook a small, explicit gap.

Opening in month 24 leaves $295,628 at that month-end. However, the minimum across the full forecast remains the first studio’s month-7 balance of $60,093. Delaying expansion creates substantial headroom at the second opening while leaving only $93 above reserve during the initial ramp. Choosing a later second lease does not repair the first location’s early funding exposure.

The later opening also produces less cash by month 36, but its second studio has traded for fewer months. That endpoint cannot establish a superior lifetime return for the earlier site. The comparison answers a sequencing question: which operating commitments fit within available funding, and what further capital is needed to preserve the chosen reserve?

Make the timetable and funding decisions together

The useful operating evidence is specific: booking availability by time slot, repeated failed booking attempts, cancellations and retention by membership cohort. Those records would test whether members exposed to constrained peak sessions actually leave more often. They would also help distinguish insufficient capacity from an unattractive timetable, instructor preferences or weak underlying retention. The assumed crowding response is the mechanism connecting this analysis; it needs validation before capital is committed on its strength.

A practical expansion decision would pair the second lease with a funded timetable for the first location and a cash forecast covering both ramps. Management could then compare a realistic instructor commitment date with the expected arrival of peak demand, rather than treating staffing and property as separate budgets. Physical scheduling and instructor availability must support the proposed classes.

The model’s month-end reserve test is only a starting point. Intramonth payment timing is absent, as are deposits, maintenance capital expenditure, tax, debt service and cannibalization between locations. With those items incorporated, the decision becomes concrete: the opening date, the capacity commitments that precede it and the funding needed through the lowest cash point.

The assumptions and exclusions below define the worked scenario.

Model notes and assumptions
  • First studio joins 30 members per month for 18 months, then 24; monthly membership price $185.
  • Baseline monthly churn is 3.5%; next-month churn adds 20 percentage points multiplied by the current fraction of unmet peak visits, capped at 20% total churn.
  • Each member requests 8 monthly visits; 60% are peak. A class accommodates 12 members and a month has 4.33 weeks.
  • Initial schedule has 30 peak and 65 total classes per week. Expanded staffing has 48 peak and 90 total classes. Instructor cost is $48 per class.
  • First studio fixed cash cost is $17,000 per month; acquisition cost is $125 per join; payment and variable servicing costs are 3.5% of revenue; fit-out is $140,000.
  • Second studio joins 26 members monthly for 18 months, then 20.8; price is $200; fixed costs $19,000 monthly; fit-out $180,000. Other calculations and instructor costs are unchanged.
  • Combined starting funding is $300,000 and reserve target is $60,000. Each studio expands staffing in operating month 13 for the opening-date comparison.

Scenario assumptions are invented for transparent public analysis; they are not client actuals, management forecasts, or US/UK market benchmarks.

Churn response to unmet peak demand is assumed rather than empirically estimated. Members continue paying the full membership fee despite some unavailable visits; the response operates through subsequent churn.

No tax, VAT, exchange-rate changes, debt service, depreciation, deposits, maintenance capex, refunds, cannibalization between locations, or separate working-capital lag.

Demand and class schedules are aggregate monthly averages. Instructor availability and physical timetable feasibility require operating validation.

Different opening dates yield different second-studio operating ages at month 36; the final cash balance is not a like-for-like lifetime return comparison.

Intramonth payment timing is not modeled; maintaining the reserve throughout a month may require more funding than the month-end shortfall.
